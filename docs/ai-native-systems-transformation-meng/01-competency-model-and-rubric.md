# 01 Competency Model And Rubric

## Competency Architecture

The program develops AI-native systems transformation professionals: graduates who first understand complex systems, then design and implement practical transformation, using AI and data as powerful enabling capabilities rather than as the sole center of the degree.

The model uses four main competency domains:

1. Systems Understanding And Diagnosis.
2. Transformation Design And Implementation.
3. AI, Data, And Digital Enablement.
4. Professional Judgment And Continuous Learning.

Human, organizational, and stakeholder integration is a cross-cutting thread. It must appear in every studio gate, major project review, portfolio, and final competency defense.

Every student must meet the common core. Students then demonstrate differentiated excellence in one or two signature areas; graduation requires Level 4 evidence in at least one of them.

## Graduate Promises

| Promise | Graduate Can Do |
|---|---|
| See the whole system | Map technical processes, data flows, human workflows, incentives, constraints, and external dependencies before proposing solutions |
| Design practical transformation | Turn diagnosis into validated interventions across process, technology, workflow, organization, and governance |
| Use AI and data responsibly | Select, integrate, and evaluate AI/data capabilities only where they improve system performance, decision quality, learning, or coordination |
| Learn continuously | Evaluate emerging AI capabilities and industry methods, update practice responsibly, and transfer learning across domains |
| Bridge people, data, and implementation | Collaborate with engineers, domain experts, managers, users, and partners to convert ideas into practical change |
| Build evidence of transformation capability | Produce portfolio, sponsor evidence, AI work logs, evaluation artifacts, implementation evidence, and defense materials |

## Domain 1: Systems Understanding And Diagnosis

This is the foundation of the program. Students must understand a system before proposing AI, data, workflow, or organizational interventions.

| Competency | Baseline Evidence |
|---|---|
| System boundary and context framing | Defines boundaries, stakeholders, incentives, constraints, risks, and external dependencies |
| Workflow, process, and data-flow mapping | Maps technical processes, human routines, data movement, organizational interfaces, and decision points |
| Root-cause and constraint diagnosis | Identifies bottlenecks, failure modes, coordination gaps, value leakage, and structural causes |
| Opportunity thesis formation | Converts messy field evidence into a focused transformation opportunity with assumptions and risks |

## Domain 2: Transformation Design And Implementation

This is the action-oriented core of the degree. Students convert diagnosis into interventions that can be tested in real contexts.

| Competency | Baseline Evidence |
|---|---|
| Intervention design | Generates options across process, technology, data, workflow, organization, and governance |
| Prototyping and pilot design | Builds practical prototypes, simulations, workflow pilots, data products, or service/process experiments matched to the project |
| Validation discipline | Tests technical feasibility, user/workflow fit, operational value, implementation constraints, and adoption risks |
| Scale, governance, and handoff planning | Produces an implementation roadmap, governance logic, adoption plan, and sponsor-facing handoff package |

## Domain 3: AI, Data, And Digital Enablement

AI and data are treated as enabling disciplines. The key question is not whether AI can be used, but where AI and data responsibly improve the system.

| Competency | Baseline Evidence |
|---|---|
| AI/data systems literacy | Explains relevant ML, LLM, agent, automation, analytics, data, API, security, and interoperability choices in project context |
| Tool selection and architecture rationale | Evaluates when AI is useful, unnecessary, risky, premature, or operationally unsuitable |
| Evaluation and responsible-use discipline | Builds evaluation harnesses, AI work logs, reliability checks, human override logic, and accountability boundaries |
| Data collaboration and governance | Defines data access, quality limits, sovereignty, compliance, incentives, privacy, and responsible use |

## Domain 4: Professional Judgment And Continuous Learning

This domain ensures that graduates are not merely technical contributors. They must make responsible decisions, communicate evidence, and renew their methods as technology and industry change.

| Competency | Baseline Evidence |
|---|---|
| Judgment under ambiguity | Makes defensible decisions with incomplete evidence, competing constraints, and changing project conditions |
| Responsibility and risk judgment | Identifies affected stakeholders, safety/privacy concerns, failure modes, ethics, and accountability boundaries |
| Sponsor and cross-boundary communication | Produces sponsor-facing summaries, technical memos, team decision records, and final defense materials |
| Adaptive learning and technology renewal | Documents how emerging tools and methods were assessed, accepted, rejected, or integrated |

## Cross-Cutting Thread: Human, Organizational, And Stakeholder Integration

Human, organizational, and stakeholder integration runs through every competency domain, every studio gate, every major project review, and the final defense.

| Students Must Repeatedly Show | Evidence Examples |
|---|---|
| Who is affected by the transformation | Stakeholder map, user interviews, affected-role analysis |
| How workflows and roles change | Before/after workflow map, decision-rights design, handoff model |
| What incentives or adoption barriers exist | Adoption-risk analysis, sponsor feedback, change-readiness evidence |
| Where accountability sits | Human override policy, responsibility memo, governance plan |
| How users, sponsors, engineers, and managers are involved | Sponsor reviews, field observations, communication records, decision logs |
| What evidence shows the intervention can work | Prototype tests, pilot data, field evidence, final sponsor board |

## Signature Excellence Areas

The common core is required for every student. Signature excellence allows students to show distinctive strength without turning the program into narrow tracks.

| Excellence Area | Distinctive Evidence |
|---|---|
| Systems Diagnosis | Strong system maps, root-cause analysis, value/risk logic, transformation opportunity thesis, field-grounded constraints |
| Transformation Design And Implementation | Validated prototype or pilot, user/workflow testing, implementation roadmap, adoption and governance evidence |
| AI/Data Systems Enablement | AI/data workflow architecture, evaluation harness, deployment logic, governance plan, responsible-use evidence |
| Practical Transformation Leadership | Sponsor coordination, stakeholder communication, project governance, adoption planning |

## Four-Level Rubric

| Level | Meaning | Evidence Standard |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Emerging | Student can explain concepts but needs heavy guidance | Course assignments and partial studio evidence |
| 2 Developing | Student can apply methods to bounded project work | Studio gate evidence with instructor correction |
| 3 Proficient | Student can integrate methods under real project ambiguity | Sponsor-facing project evidence and defensible decisions |
| 4 Distinctive | Student shows leadership or excellence in a signature area | Evidence survives faculty/sponsor questioning and shows independent leadership, transferability, or distinctive contribution in a signature area |

## Minimum Graduation Standard

Each graduate must reach Level 3 in all common-core competency domains and Level 4 in at least one signature excellence area. Final judgment is based on project evidence, sponsor-facing artifacts, portfolio materials, and the competency defense.
